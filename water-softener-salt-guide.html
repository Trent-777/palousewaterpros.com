Water Softener Salt: Which Kind, How Much, and What Goes Wrong

By Trent, owner of Palouse Water Pros · Moscow, Idaho · September 2026

Once a softener is installed, salt is the one part of it you deal with yourself. A lot of the softener questions I get around Moscow and Latah County come back to the brine tank: which bag to buy, how much salt it should be using, why the level hasn't moved in two months, or why the water feels hard again with a full tank. Here's what I tell people in person.

If you're still deciding whether you need a softener at all, start with water softeners in Moscow, Idaho instead. This page assumes you already have one.

The salt types, and what you actually get for the money

Use only salt made for water softeners — not table salt, road salt, or ice melt. Among true softener salts, the main difference is purity: how much insoluble material comes along with the sodium chloride. That material doesn't dissolve; it settles in the bottom of the brine tank as sediment, and over time it's what clogs things up.

TypeWhat it isHonest trade-off
Rock saltMined salt, crushed and screenedLeast expensive and the least pure. Carries the most insoluble minerals, so the brine tank builds up sediment and needs cleaning out more often.
Solar saltSalt water evaporated in outdoor ponds, sold as crystals or pelletsMuch purer than rock salt and a reasonable middle ground. Still leaves a bit more residue than evaporated salt.
Evaporated pelletsPurified brine evaporated with heat, pressed into pelletsThe purest sodium chloride option and the least residue. Less prone to bridging and mushing. Costs more per bag than rock salt.
Potassium chlorideA sodium-free regenerant that works in most standard softenersAdds no sodium to the water. Costs noticeably more and is less efficient pound for pound, so the softener usually goes through more of it.

Why purity matters more on iron-bearing well water

On a lot of Palouse wells, the softener isn't just removing hardness. As I explain in the Palouse well water guide, a properly configured softener can take out moderate dissolved iron and manganese along with the calcium and magnesium. So the resin is already working harder than it would on city water, and every regeneration needs to be a good one. Low-purity salt adds sediment that can foul the brine valve and the small passages the brine is drawn through, and a softener that misses a good regeneration on iron water starts building metals up on the resin.

On iron water I recommend evaporated pellets, or solar salt at minimum. Some brands also sell pellets with an iron-fighting additive meant to help keep iron off the resin between cleanings. Those can help, but they're a supplement to a correctly set-up softener and periodic resin cleaning, not a replacement. If iron is heavy enough that it needs its own treatment stage, see iron and manganese removal.

Potassium chloride for sodium-restricted households

A salt-based softener adds a small amount of sodium in exchange for the hardness it removes — harder raw water, more sodium. For most people that isn't a concern, but for households watching sodium on a doctor's advice, potassium chloride is the usual swap in most standard softeners.

The trade-offs are cost and efficiency. Potassium chloride costs considerably more per bag, and because it isn't as efficient as sodium chloride, the softener generally needs more of it to regenerate the same resin — so the salt setting often has to be raised when you switch. Check the manual rather than just changing bags. One caution from Health Canada's guidance: people with kidney problems or on medications that affect potassium should talk to their doctor before drinking potassium-softened water. For drinking water specifically, another common approach is plain salt for the house plus a reverse osmosis system at the kitchen sink, which removes most of the added sodium from the water you drink.

How much salt does a water softener use?

There isn't one honest number for this, because salt use depends on what your water carries and how the softener is programmed. The drivers:

  • Hardness. More grains per gallon means the resin fills up faster and regenerates more often.
  • Iron and manganese. In softener sizing, each part per million of iron or manganese counts as several grains of hardness. A well with a little iron uses more salt than the hardness number alone suggests.
  • People and water use. More showers, laundry, and dishes means more gallons through the resin between regenerations.
  • Regeneration settings. How much salt goes in per regeneration (softener design references put the typical dose between about 6 and 15 pounds per cubic foot of resin) and what triggers a regeneration. Lower doses are more salt-efficient; higher doses restore more capacity. A metered softener generally wastes less than one on a fixed timer that regenerates whether it needs to or not.

More useful than any number is your own pattern. Once you know how fast your household normally goes through salt, a change tells you something. Salt disappearing much faster can mean higher water use, a leak, or a setting problem. Salt that stops going down usually means a bridge, a stuck brine valve, or a unit that isn't regenerating.

How full to keep the brine tank

You don't need to pack the brine tank to the rim. Your manual comes first, but the general guidance is consistent: keep it at least a quarter full, around half is plenty, and always keep dry salt visible above the water line. Standing water with no salt above it means weak brine and a short regeneration. Overfilling gives salt more chance to cake together, especially somewhere humid or with temperature swings, like a garage or shop.

Salt bridging and mushing: how to spot and fix them

A salt bridge is a hard crust across the tank above the water. From the top it looks full; underneath is an air pocket. Water can't reach the salt, so the softener regenerates on weak brine or plain water. The classic sign is salt that hasn't gone down in weeks while the water slowly turns hard again.

The broom-handle check: every time you add salt, take a broom handle and push it gently straight down through the salt. It should slide all the way to the bottom of the tank. If it stops on something hard partway down that isn't the floor or the wall of the tank, you've found a bridge. Put the softener in bypass, then tap the crust gently but firmly with the handle until it breaks up — don't pry against the tank walls or hit the brine well. Scoop out any big chunks, let things settle, and then run a manual regeneration so the resin gets a proper brine.

Mushing is the other failure. Salt that dissolves and recrystallizes can form a thick sludge in the bottom of the tank that blocks brine from being drawn. It's more likely with lower-purity salt, humidity, and temperature swings. A bridge you can break up in place; mush generally means cleaning out the tank: bypass and unplug the softener, scoop or siphon out the old water and sludge, rinse the tank, refill with fresh, high-purity salt, and run a manual regeneration. Higher-purity salt is the best prevention for both problems.

Resin cleaner on iron water

Salt regeneration doesn't fully strip iron and manganese off softener resin. On iron-bearing well water some stays behind each cycle, coating the beads and cutting capacity until the softener can't keep up. A resin cleaner made for softeners, added per the label and run through a regeneration, removes that buildup. More iron means more frequent cleaning. If the softener has to be cleaned constantly to keep up, the iron load may need its own stage upstream.

Signs the softener isn't working

  • Soap that stops lathering well and a filmy feel after showering.
  • Spots on dishes and new white scale on faucets and shower glass.
  • On a well, orange or black staining coming back in toilets and fixtures.
  • Salt that hasn't gone down in weeks — or is disappearing much faster than normal.

Check it with an inexpensive hardness test strip. Run a softened cold tap for 30 seconds, dip, and read the strip at the time the package says — reading late makes it look harder. Compare against an unsoftened tap, such as an outside hose bib if yours is on raw water. Softened water should read at or near zero. If the treated tap reads close to raw, check the bypass valve, look for a bridge, confirm salt above the water line, and check the clock and settings after any power outage.

When to call

Salt and bridges are yours to handle. Call when the strip still reads hard after you've ruled out a bridge, when the brine tank is filling with water or overflowing, when the unit doesn't seem to regenerate, when small resin beads show up in faucet aerators, or when iron staining keeps coming back on a well. Those point to a valve, setting, or resin problem — or a softener never sized for the iron load.
