Water Testing in Moscow, Idaho & Latah County

If you're looking for water testing in Moscow, Idaho, you probably have a reason — orange stains in the tub, black slime in the toilet tank, a rotten-egg smell, scale on the faucets, a house you're about to buy, or a treatment system that isn't doing its job. I'm Trent, owner of Palouse Water Pros, and I'm based right here in Moscow. I come out to homes in town and across Latah County and the Idaho side of the Palouse, test the water on-site in front of you, and tell you plainly what I find. Private well, city water, or a house that isn't built yet — the process starts the same way.

What the on-site visit looks like

Most visits run 45 to 75 minutes. I set up at a tap — usually the kitchen, and if you already have equipment, ideally somewhere I can sample before it too — and run the tests right there. You watch the results come up, and I explain what each number means for your house as we go. Nothing gets carried off to be interpreted somewhere you can't see it.

Then I look at the plumbing: where the water comes in, the pressure tank, the water heater, any existing softener or filter, and the space you'd have for equipment if it turns out you need some. Treatment has to fit the house — floor space, a drain, power, room to service it — so I'd rather see that on the first visit than guess at it later. I spent years as a general contractor doing a lot of plumbing, so this part goes quickly.

The rest is a relaxed conversation. What have you noticed, and for how long? How many people are in the house? What bothers you most — the stains, the taste, the smell, the scale on the dishwasher? There's no pitch at the end of it.

What I test on-site

The core numbers are the ones that drive most treatment decisions on the Palouse, where low-oxygen groundwater in the basalt aquifers tends to pick up iron and manganese, and hardness rides along:

  • Hardness — measured in grains per gallon. Around 7 gpg and up is hard water: scale, spotty dishes, a water heater working harder than it should.
  • Iron — starts staining at about 0.3 mg/L.
  • Manganese — stains at much lower levels, around 0.05 mg/L, and 0.3 mg/L is EPA health-advisory territory. It's often the one that gets missed.
  • pH — below roughly 6.8 or above roughly 8, it changes which treatment approaches will actually work.
  • Whatever else the situation calls for — depending on what I see, smell, and hear from you, I may check a few more things, like dissolved solids, color and tannins, odor-related compounds, or chlorine on city water.

What goes to a lab, and why

Some things shouldn't be answered with a field test. Bacteria is the big one: a coliform test needs a sterile sample, careful handling, and a certified lab to get a result you can trust. Any positive coliform result matters — it changes the whole treatment design, so I want it known up front, not discovered after equipment is installed. If it comes back positive, here's what to do next.

The same goes for anything unusual — if the on-site numbers don't add up, if there's a health question like nitrate or arsenic, or if you need documented results for a real estate transaction, a lab is the right tool. I'll tell you which lab tests actually make sense for your situation and why, rather than handing you a long menu. When results come back, I'll go through them with you. Before the visit

A little prep makes the visit more useful:

  • Clear a path to the pressure tank, water heater, and any existing equipment.
  • If you have a softener or filter, leave it running normally — don't bypass it before I get there.
  • Pull together any past test results, the well log or drilling paperwork if you have it, and manuals or receipts for existing equipment.
  • If more than one person makes decisions about the house, it helps if they can be there for the conversation.
Jot this down before you call: Is the problem in the hot water, the cold, or both? (Odor that only shows up in hot water often points at the water heater, not the well.) Which fixtures — every tap, or just one bathroom? When did it start, and does it change with the season? Find any past water tests, even old ones. And snap a photo of your existing equipment, including the label on the control head. Those few details save time and often narrow things down before I arrive.
