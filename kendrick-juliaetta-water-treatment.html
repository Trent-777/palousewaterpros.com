What wells run into here

This corner of Latah County is different from the rolling wheat ground around Moscow. Kendrick and Juliaetta sit down in the canyon along the Potlatch River, with Highway 3 following the river between them, while the farms and homes up on the ridges — Bear Ridge, Texas Ridge, Potlatch Ridge, Fix Ridge and the rest — sit hundreds of feet higher. That difference in elevation matters for water.

Across the Idaho side of the Palouse, most private wells draw from fractured basalt and the sediment layers sandwiched between basalt flows. A well drilled on a ridge top has to go down through far more ground before it finds water, and it may end up pulling from a different water-bearing zone than a well on the canyon floor a mile or two away. A well close to the river bottom may be shallower and sit in different ground entirely. So two neighbors in the same zip code can have water that looks nothing alike, and a test result from down the road doesn't tell you much about yours.

What I typically look for on wells around Kendrick and Juliaetta:

  • Iron — orange-red staining in sinks, toilets, and laundry. Low-oxygen groundwater dissolves iron out of the rock, and staining usually starts showing up at about 0.3 mg/L.
  • Manganese — black or gray staining, dark slime in toilet tanks, a bitter or metallic taste. It stains at much lower levels than iron (around 0.05 mg/L), and at 0.3 mg/L it's in EPA health-advisory territory. It's also harder to remove, which is why a lot of generic "iron filters" disappoint.
  • Hardness — scale on fixtures, faucet aerators, and water heater elements, spotty dishes, dry skin. Anything over about 7 grains per gallon counts as hard.
  • pH — water below roughly 6.8 or above about 8 changes which removal methods will work, so it has to be measured before anything gets designed.
  • Coliform bacteria — worth knowing about on any private well, and especially on shallower wells, older casings, or wells that haven't been tested in years. A positive result changes the whole design, so I want to know about it at the start, not after equipment is on the wall.

If one of these stands out, I've written more about it on its own page: iron and manganese removal, water softeners and hard water, and rotten-egg or sulfur smell.

Older homes, small acreage, and hobby orchards

Both towns go back to the late 1800s — the railroad reached Kendrick in 1891, and Juliaetta's valley and surrounding ridges were known early on for fruit orchards and big gardens. A lot of what I see out here reflects that history: older houses that have been added onto a few times, small acreages with a shop and a garden, and properties where the well, the pressure tank, and the plumbing were put in by different people in different decades.

That kind of house brings its own questions, and they're worth answering before buying equipment:

  • Where does the water actually go? On a property with a garden, orchard trees, livestock waterers, or a shop, it usually doesn't make sense to soften or filter every gallon. Splitting off outdoor and irrigation lines ahead of treatment can shrink the system you need and cut down on salt and media use.
  • What's already there? I often find an old softener or filter that's been bypassed, or a system that was sized for a different house. Sometimes it can be put back to work; sometimes it's the reason the water still stains.
  • Is there room to do it right? Older mechanical rooms and well houses can be tight. I design around the space, the plumbing, and the flow rate the well can actually deliver, so the system fits and can be serviced later.
  • Buying or selling? A basic lender test often covers bacteria and maybe nitrate, and skips iron, manganese, hardness, and pH. If you're purchasing a place out here, a fuller picture before closing helps you budget for treatment instead of finding out after you move in.

If your home is on a town water system rather than a private well, the questions are simpler — usually hardness, taste, and whether you want a reverse osmosis system for drinking water. The same testing-first process applies; the job is usually smaller.

How to check at home before you call: look at the inside of your toilet tank lid and the tank itself. Orange-brown buildup points toward iron; black or dark gray slime points toward manganese. Check your showerhead and faucet aerators for white, crusty scale, which points to hardness. Note whether the problem is on hot water only, cold only, or both, and whether it got worse after a wet spring or a dry late summer. Snap a photo or two. That five-minute look makes our first conversation much more useful — and it helps me bring the right test gear.

How it works

Same process everywhere I work. It starts with a short phone call or text so I understand what you're seeing. Then I come out for an on-site visit — usually 45 to 75 minutes — and test the water in front of you so you can see the results for yourself. From there you get a clear recommendation, either one option or a couple of options with the tradeoffs spelled out, followed by a written proposal. If you move forward, I build and install the system myself, and I'll give you an honest timeline for your specific job up front.

Everything I install uses industry-standard, serviceable components — no proprietary lock-in, so you're never stuck with parts only one company can get. My goal is never too much and never too little: often a properly configured softener can handle moderate iron, manganese, and hardness in one pass, and heavier iron may call for oxidation and filtration instead. I don't sell salt-free "conditioners," magnetic descalers, or one-size-fits-all online packages. Test first, design around the numbers, install it clean, and verify the results afterward.
