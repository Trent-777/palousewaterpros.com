What wells run into out here

This end of Latah County sits where Palouse loess meets timbered mountain ground, and the wells reflect it. What I routinely find on jobs in this area:

  • High manganese — often the dominant problem out here, not just a sidekick to iron. Black slime in toilet tanks, gray staining, bitter taste. It's harder to remove than iron and defeats most off-the-shelf "iron filters."
  • Iron — the familiar orange-red staining, frequently riding along with the manganese.
  • Hardness — scale on fixtures and water heaters, dry skin, spotted dishes.
  • Coliform bacteria — common on older wells and shallow casings in this area, especially after wet springs. It changes the whole treatment design, so it needs to be known up front, not discovered later.
  • Organics and tannins — timber-country ground can tint water and bind iron in ways that make standard filters underperform. This is a frequent reason a previous system "never really worked."
Real example: a recent job on Garden Gulch Road near Princeton came back with high manganese, hardness, and a positive coliform history — the full corridor combination. The fix wasn't one magic filter; it was a treatment train designed in the right order for those specific numbers. That's the norm out here, not the exception.

Buying property out here?

The basic test a lender requires often skips manganese, pH, and tannins entirely — the exact things most likely to be wrong on this corridor. A complete picture before closing gives you negotiating leverage and a real treatment budget instead of a surprise. I can usually turn testing around fast enough for a closing timeline, and I work with realtors on rural transactions regularly.
