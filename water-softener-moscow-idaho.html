Signs your water is hard

Hardness is mostly dissolved calcium and magnesium. It's invisible in the glass, but it leaves evidence around the house:

  • White, chalky scale on faucets, showerheads, and glass shower doors.
  • Spotted dishes and cloudy glassware coming out of the dishwasher.
  • Soap that won't lather and a filmy feel after a shower; dry skin and hair.
  • Water heater trouble — popping or rumbling noises as scale builds up on the bottom of the tank.
  • Stiff, dingy laundry even when you use more detergent.

What "grains per gallon" means — and where hard starts

Softener people measure hardness in grains per gallon (gpg). One grain per gallon equals about 17.1 milligrams per liter (the same as parts per million), so a lab report in mg/L converts easily. The Water Quality Association calls water hard at 7 gpg and very hard above 10.5 gpg. The U.S. Geological Survey uses similar lines in mg/L: 61–120 is moderately hard, 121–180 is hard, and over 180 is very hard. At 7 gpg or above, a softener is usually worth it. Below that it's a judgment call, and I'll tell you honestly if you don't need one.

What homes around Moscow run into

Most rural wells around here draw from fractured basalt aquifers or the sediments between basalt flows. That low-oxygen groundwater carries hardness, and it very often carries dissolved iron and manganese right along with it. That combination is what makes well water softening here different from softening in a lot of other places. What I typically find:

  • Well water: hardness plus some iron and manganese, sometimes with pH or tannins that complicate things. A softener can often handle the hardness and moderate dissolved iron and manganese in one pass — but only if it's built and programmed for that job. Heavier iron may need its own stage first; see iron and manganese removal.
  • City water: a much simpler softening job — no iron load to plan around, and the chemistry is consistent. The City of Moscow publishes an annual water quality report, and I still test at your tap so the softener is set to what's actually coming in. More on in-town homes on the Moscow city water page.

Why sizing matters — and why default settings fail on well water

A softener's job is to capture a certain number of grains between regenerations. Sizing it means working from your hardness, your household's water use, and — on a well — your iron and manganese. In standard sizing math, each part per million of iron or manganese counts as several grains of hardness, because those metals load and foul the resin much faster than calcium does. Leave them out and the unit is undersized from day one.

This is where big-box softeners go wrong on Palouse well water. Out of the box they're set for plain hardness: a generic hardness number, a salt dose aimed at efficiency, and regeneration timed for a city-water house. On a well carrying iron and manganese, that setup lets metals build up in the resin bed faster than each regeneration clears them. The resin fouls and dies young. The fix isn't a bigger box; it's the right resin volume, salt dose, regeneration frequency, and — where needed — protection upstream. Too small wastes your money on a unit that can't keep up; too big wastes salt and water. Never too much, never too little.

Salt-based softener or salt-free "conditioner"?

A salt-based softener uses ion exchange: resin beads trade sodium (or potassium) for the calcium and magnesium in your water, and a regeneration cycle with brine flushes the hardness down the drain. The hardness is actually removed, and you can measure it at the tap afterward.

Salt-free "conditioners" don't remove hardness. The better ones use a process called template-assisted crystallization, which changes how the minerals form scale rather than taking them out of the water. On city water with modest hardness, that can reduce some scaling. On genuinely hard Palouse well water with iron and manganese present, those metals coat the media and it stops doing its job. I skip them, and I skip magnetic and electronic descalers too — there's no credible evidence they do anything on water like ours.

Softener plus reverse osmosis for drinking water

A softener makes water better for the house, not purer to drink. It swaps hardness for a small amount of sodium and doesn't address other dissolved minerals. A lot of households pair a whole-house softener with a reverse osmosis system at the kitchen sink. Softened water protects the RO membrane from scale, and the RO takes out the added sodium along with most other dissolved solids. If you're watching your sodium intake, that combination — or potassium chloride in place of salt — is worth talking through.

Check before you buy: an inexpensive hardness test strip from the hardware store will give you a rough gpg number in a minute. If you're on city water, the city's annual water quality report is another starting point. On a well, also lift the lid on a toilet tank: orange film points to iron, black slime to manganese. If you see either, a softener chosen off a hardness number alone is the wrong starting point — the metals need to be in the design.

Where a softener goes and what a clean install looks like

A softener belongs where the water line enters the house — after the pressure tank on a well, and ahead of the water heater so the heater gets soft water. A clean install is one you can live with for years:

  • A bypass valve so water keeps running while the unit is serviced.
  • A proper drain with an air gap, and a brine tank overflow line that goes somewhere sensible.
  • Thought given to outside faucets — irrigation and hose bibs usually don't need softened water.
  • Industry-standard valves and resin, so any competent tech can service it down the road. No proprietary lock-in.
  • Programmed on-site from your test results, then verified with a test of the softened water.

Living with a softener: salt and occasional service

Day to day, the main job is keeping salt in the brine tank. Check it every few weeks until you learn how fast your household goes through it, and break up any hard crust ("bridging") that forms above the water line. Beyond that, a softener wants occasional attention: a hardness check at the tap to confirm it's still working, and on iron-bearing well water, periodic resin cleaning and a look at the settings. If your water or household changes, the programming should change with it. Salt types, bridging, and a maintenance checklist are in the softener salt guide.
