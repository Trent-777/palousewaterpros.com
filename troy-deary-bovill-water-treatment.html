What wells run into along Highway 8

This stretch of Latah County is a transition zone, and you can see it out the windshield. Around Troy it's still mostly rolling Palouse farm ground. Past Troy, Highway 8 bends northeast toward Deary and Bovill, and the country turns to timber — this is the wooded northeastern corner of the county where the logging and railroad towns grew up. The ground underneath changes too. The basalt layers that hold much of the Palouse's groundwater thin out toward the east, and around Deary and Bovill wells can draw from granite and older metamorphic rock overlain by clay, sand, and gravel. Wells a few miles apart can come back looking nothing alike, which is exactly why I don't recommend anything before testing.

What I typically find on wells along this corridor:

  • Iron — orange-red staining in sinks, toilets, and laundry, and a metallic taste. Low-oxygen groundwater dissolves it out of the rock, and once it hits air it shows up on your fixtures. Staining usually starts around 0.3 mg/L.
  • Manganese — black or gray staining, dark slime in the toilet tank, a bitter edge to the water. It stains at much lower levels than iron (around 0.05 mg/L), and at 0.3 mg/L it's in EPA health-advisory territory. It's also harder to remove, and it's the reason a lot of off-the-shelf "iron filters" disappoint.
  • Hardness — white scale on faucets and showerheads, water heaters that lose efficiency, spotted dishes. Anything over about 7 grains per gallon counts as hard.
  • Coliform bacteria — more likely on older wells, shallow or damaged casings, and after wet springs. Any positive result matters, and it changes the order of the whole treatment train, so I want to know about it up front.
  • Organics and tannins — more common as you get into the timber, where water can pick up a tea-colored tint. Tannins can bind iron in a way that lets it slip right through a standard filter.

pH matters too. If it's below roughly 6.8 or above about 8, oxidation-based iron and manganese removal gets less reliable, and the design has to account for it. For a deeper look at these two metals, see my page on iron and manganese removal.

On city water in Troy, Deary, or Bovill?

Troy, Deary, and Bovill each run their own small city water systems, and like other community water systems in Idaho they publish an annual water quality report you can ask the city for. If you're hooked up to one of them, your needs are usually simpler than a private well — often a question of hardness, taste, or drinking water quality at the kitchen tap. A properly sized softener or an under-sink reverse osmosis system may be all you need, and sometimes the honest answer is that you need nothing at all. Outside town limits, most homes along the corridor are on their own wells, and that's where the real design work happens.

Older farmhouses, timber-country wells, and property purchases

A lot of the homes out here have history. Farmhouses around Troy have often been on the same well for decades, with plumbing that's been patched and added onto by several owners. Cabins and homes in the timber toward Deary and Bovill sometimes have wells nobody has tested in years, or treatment equipment left behind by a previous owner that no one quite understands. Before I size anything, I look at what's already there — the well, the pressure tank, the plumbing, any old equipment — because sometimes the fix is simpler than replacing everything, and sometimes the old equipment is part of the problem.

If you're buying rural property along Highway 8, water deserves a close look before closing. The basic test a lender asks for often covers only bacteria and nitrate, and skips iron, manganese, hardness, pH, and tannins — the things most likely to affect whether you like living with the water. Knowing the full picture ahead of time gives you a realistic treatment budget and something concrete to bring to the negotiation, instead of a surprise the first week you move in. Tell me your closing date when you reach out, and I'll give you a straight answer about whether the timeline works.

Before you call — two things worth checking: First, lift the toilet tank lid. Orange-red staining points to iron; black or gray slime points to manganese; white crust points to hardness. Second, look up your well log. The Idaho Department of Water Resources keeps well driller's reports that often list well depth, what the driller hit on the way down, and water level. If you can find yours, it helps me understand your well before I ever show up.

How it works

Same process everywhere I go: a short phone or text conversation, then an on-site visit — usually 45 to 75 minutes — with testing done in front of you so you see the numbers yourself. From there you get a clear recommendation, either one option or a couple to compare, and a written proposal. I build and install the system myself, and I give you an honest timeline up front for your specific job. Everything I install uses industry-standard, serviceable components — no proprietary lock-in. Often a properly configured softener can handle moderate iron, manganese, and hardness in one pass; heavier iron calls for oxidation and filtration; carbon plays a supporting role. What I don't sell: salt-free "conditioners," magnetic descalers, or one-size packages built without a test. Never too much, never too little.
