Well Water Smells Like Rotten Eggs? Sulfur Odor Treatment in Moscow, Idaho

If your well water smells like rotten eggs — or just musty or swampy — you're smelling hydrogen sulfide gas. It's one of the more unpleasant things a well can do, and it tends to get blamed on the wrong part of the house. I'm Trent, based in Moscow, and I come out to homes on wells around Moscow, Viola, Troy, Genesee, Deary, Potlatch, and the rest of Latah County to figure out where the smell is actually coming from. The answer decides whether the fix is a simple part in your water heater or real treatment on the well itself.

What causes the smell

Hydrogen sulfide is a gas that dissolves in water. Most people can smell it at very low levels — extension and state health sources put the detection point around 0.5 mg/L or below, with the classic rotten-egg smell showing up around 1–2 mg/L. It usually gets into well water one of two ways:

  • Sulfur-reducing bacteria. These are naturally occurring bacteria that live in low-oxygen places — deep in the aquifer, in the well, or in plumbing — and convert sulfate in the water into hydrogen sulfide. They aren't considered a health threat themselves, but they're the most common source of the smell.
  • A reaction inside the water heater. Most tank water heaters have a magnesium anode rod that protects the tank from corrosion. In water that contains sulfate, that rod can help drive the conversion of sulfate to hydrogen sulfide — and the warm tank gives sulfur-reducing bacteria a comfortable place to grow.

Beyond the smell, hydrogen sulfide is corrosive. It attacks iron, steel, copper, and brass, tarnishes silverware, and can leave yellow or black stains on fixtures.

Hot water only, or hot and cold?

This is the single most useful thing to know before anyone sells you equipment:

  • Smell in the hot water only almost always points to the water heater — the anode rod, bacteria living in the tank, or both. The well itself may be fine. The smell is often worst after the heater has sat unused for a while, or when the thermostat is turned down.
  • Smell in both hot and cold means it's coming from the well, the aquifer, or the plumbing between them. That's a water treatment question, not a water heater question.
How to check at home: fill a clean glass with cold water, carry it into another room away from the sink, and smell it. Then do the same with hot water. Doing it away from the sink matters — a smelly drain can fool you into thinking the water is the problem. No smell in either glass usually means the drain. Smell in the hot glass only points to the water heater. Smell in both points to the well. Note which faucets you tried and whether the odor is stronger first thing in the morning; that's useful information when I come out.

Why sulfur often travels with iron and manganese here

Most rural wells around Moscow and the Palouse draw from the Columbia River basalts — the shallower Wanapum and the deeper Grande Ronde — or from the sediments between basalt flows. As I explain in the Palouse well water guide, groundwater in these formations tends to be low in oxygen, which is exactly why iron and manganese dissolve out of the rock and stay dissolved until they reach your fixtures. Low oxygen is also the condition sulfur-reducing bacteria need. So a well with a sulfur smell is a well worth testing for iron and manganese too, and the reverse.

That combination matters for treatment. Hydrogen sulfide can react with dissolved iron to form black particles, it can foul equipment that was sized only for iron, and the oxidation step that removes it also affects iron and manganese. If you're already dealing with staining, my iron and manganese removal page covers that side of it.

How sulfur odor is treated

There's no single "sulfur filter" that fits every well. The right approach depends on where the smell starts, how strong it is, and what else is in the water:

  • Water heater anode swap. When only the hot water smells, replacing the magnesium anode with a different material (aluminum or a powered anode, for example) often solves it. Pulling the anode out entirely is not the answer — it shortens the life of the heater. Flushing and disinfecting the tank may be part of the fix; follow the heater manufacturer's guidance before putting chlorine in it.
  • Shock chlorination of the well. A strong chlorine dose through the well and plumbing kills sulfur-reducing bacteria in the well and lines. It's a reasonable first step and sometimes enough, but extension sources are clear that the odor often returns within days or weeks, because the bacteria live in the aquifer itself, not just the casing.
  • Oxidation plus filtration. The most common permanent treatment: oxidize the hydrogen sulfide (with air, chlorine, or another oxidant) and catch what's left in a filter media bed. On Palouse water, this has to be designed alongside the iron, manganese, and pH numbers — the same caveats I describe in the guide apply here.
  • Activated carbon. Carbon can handle low levels of hydrogen sulfide and is often used as a polishing stage after oxidation. On its own it gets overwhelmed quickly at higher concentrations.
  • Continuous disinfection. For stronger or persistent odor, a metered chlorine feed with enough contact time — usually followed by carbon to remove the leftover chlorine — handles hydrogen sulfide and keeps bacteria in check. It also interacts with softener resin and the rest of the treatment train, so it needs to be planned, not bolted on.

Which of these makes sense is decided by the on-site test, not by the smell alone. The same rotten-egg odor can be a part swap in one house and a full treatment train in the next.

When to worry

At the levels that show up in household water, hydrogen sulfide is mainly a nuisance — the U.S. EPA doesn't regulate it in drinking water, largely because water becomes too unpleasant to drink well before it reaches levels of concern. Sulfate, which often comes with it, has an EPA secondary (non-health) guideline of 250 mg/L; above that it can have a laxative effect.

The bigger concern is what else might be going on. A smell that appears suddenly, changes, or shows up along with cloudiness is a reason to have the well tested for coliform bacteria. Sulfur bacteria aren't the same thing as coliform, but a well with a new problem deserves a bacteria test, and any positive coliform result changes the treatment design. If you're buying a house with a well that smells — or reopening a cabin or rental that sat empty — test before you assume it's just the water heater.
