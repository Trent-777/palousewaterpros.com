Whether you're on City of Moscow water or a private well out in Latah County, an under-sink RO system can give you clean, good-tasting water for drinking, coffee, and cooking. What reverse osmosis removes — and what it doesn't

Reverse osmosis pushes water through a very fine membrane that holds back most dissolved material and sends it down the drain. According to the CDC, RO systems remove chemicals such as lead, copper, chromium, chloride, and sodium, and may also reduce arsenic, fluoride, radium, sulfate, calcium, magnesium, nitrate, and more. The CDC also rates RO as highly effective against bacteria, viruses, and parasites.

It isn't a cure-all, and it's worth knowing the gaps before you buy:

  • Chlorine and many organic chemicals are handled mostly by the carbon stages, not the membrane. Small, volatile compounds and dissolved gases can slip through a membrane, which is why a good system is built in stages.
  • Hydrogen sulfide — the rotten-egg smell — is a gas, and RO is a poor tool for it. That gets solved upstream; see my page on sulfur smell in well water.
  • A contaminated well is not fixed by one faucet. If a well tests positive for coliform, the whole house needs a proper disinfection design; an RO at the kitchen sink doesn't protect the shower, the bathroom sink, or the ice in your freezer if it isn't plumbed to it.

Every system is different, so check what a specific unit is certified to reduce rather than assuming.

Why RO is a drinking-water system, not a whole-house one

RO is slow and uses extra water to do its job. A conventional point-of-use system can send five gallons or more down the drain for every gallon it makes, according to the EPA; newer WaterSense-labeled units are held to 2.3 gallons or less per gallon. That's a sensible trade for the few gallons a day you drink and cook with. It makes no sense for showers, laundry, and toilets, where you don't need that level of treatment. So the usual approach is to treat the whole house for what affects the whole house — hardness, iron, manganese, sediment — and put RO at the one faucet where purity matters.

What homes run into here

On Palouse wells, the membrane is only as good as the water feeding it. Basalt-aquifer wells here commonly carry iron, manganese, and hardness, and all three are hard on RO membranes: hardness scales the membrane surface, and iron and manganese oxidize and deposit on it. A membrane fed raw well water with those numbers tends to lose production and rejection far sooner than it should. That's why, on most wells, I recommend the RO go downstream of a properly set up water softener or iron and manganese removal. The whole-house equipment protects your fixtures and appliances; the RO then polishes drinking water from an easy feed instead of fighting the raw well.

On City of Moscow water, the supply comes from wells in the Wanapum and Grande Ronde basalt aquifers, and the city uses chlorine in its treatment. The water is regulated and tested, so most city customers who want RO are after taste, less chlorine flavor, and lower dissolved solids — better coffee and tea, clearer ice, less spotting on the kettle. That's a perfectly good reason. City water does need a solid carbon prefilter ahead of the membrane, because chlorine damages the thin-film membranes most RO systems use. If you're on city water and weighing whole-house options too, my Moscow city water page covers that side.

Fridge and ice maker lines

A lot of people want RO water in the refrigerator door and ice maker, not just at the sink. It can be done, but it has to be planned. An RO system delivers water at lower pressure than your main line, especially once the storage tank draws down, and some ice makers and dispensers are fussy about pressure. Long tubing runs to the fridge make it worse. Depending on the layout, that can mean larger tubing, a pressure-boosting option on the RO, or bypassing the fridge's own filter so water isn't being filtered twice. I sort that out during the site visit instead of discovering it after the drywall is closed up.

Remineralization — optional

Because RO strips out most dissolved minerals, the water can taste flat to some people. A remineralization cartridge after the membrane adds a small amount of calcium and magnesium back in. Some people like it, some prefer the water straight. It's an option, not a requirement, and I'll tell you honestly whether I think it's worth it for your setup.

What upkeep looks like

An RO system is not install-and-forget. The prefilters (sediment and carbon) and any post-filter are replaced on a regular schedule, and the membrane lasts considerably longer than the cartridges — how much longer depends heavily on the water feeding it and whether pretreatment is doing its job. Heavily loaded feed water shortens every interval. I use standard cartridges and housings that you can buy from more than one source, and I'll walk you through what gets changed and roughly how often for your particular water before you decide.

A quick check at home: if you already have an RO, look at the faucet flow and the tank. If the faucet dribbles after a night of no use, the tank feels heavy but little comes out, or the water has started tasting "off," the prefilters or membrane may be past due — or the feed water is fouling the membrane faster than expected. On a well, the second possibility is worth testing for before you just buy another membrane.

A proper install vs. a big-box kit

The kits on the shelf aren't bad membranes. The trouble is everything around them. A kit doesn't know if your well has iron, manganese, or hardness that will foul the membrane in short order, or whether your pressure is high enough for it to work well. Many come with a self-piercing saddle valve for the feed and a drain connection left up to the installer — two places where leaks and backflow problems tend to start. A proper install means a real shutoff on the feed, a correct air-gap or drain connection, tubing routed and secured so nothing kinks, a cleanly mounted faucet, pretreatment that matches your water, and a check afterward to make sure it's actually doing what it should.
