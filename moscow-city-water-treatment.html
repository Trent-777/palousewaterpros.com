Where Moscow's city water comes from

Moscow runs entirely on groundwater. According to the City of Moscow's Water Department, the city's wells draw from two basalt aquifers: the shallower Wanapum and the deeper Grande Ronde, which the city describes as its main source. Those same aquifers supply much of the Palouse Basin, and their water levels are tracked by the Palouse Basin Aquifer Committee, a group that includes the City of Moscow, Latah County, and the University of Idaho along with its neighbors across the basin.

A few things follow from that, and they're all stated in the city's own materials:

  • It's disinfected and regulated. The city is a regulated public drinking water system, samples monthly through a third-party lab, and publishes an annual Consumer Confidence Report. The most recent one posted on the city's Water Quality Reports page is the 2024 annual water quality report (covering the 2024 testing period). If you want the official numbers for your system, that report is the place to start.
  • It's chlorinated. The city uses chlorine, and at the Wanapum wells it's paired with potassium permanganate and greensand filters to pull out iron and manganese before the water goes into the system.
  • Iron and manganese are part of the picture. The city's water department notes that its basalt wells naturally contain iron and manganese. Some of it reacts with chlorine and forms a reddish floc that settles in the mains and can stir back up when flow is high. That's why the city flushes the system twice a year, and why you may occasionally see brown-tinged water after a hydrant is opened nearby. The city describes this as an aesthetic issue, not a health one.

What the city's water treatment does not do is soften. Hardness — dissolved calcium and magnesium the water picks up moving through basalt — goes straight to your tap. I won't quote you a single hardness number for "Moscow water," because I'd rather measure yours than repeat a figure from somewhere else. It's enough hardness that scale, spotting, and water heater buildup are the most common complaints I hear from city customers, and it's the reason softeners are so common in town.

The usual city-water setup: softener plus RO

On city water, you aren't fighting coliform or heavy well iron. The job is narrower, and the equipment should be too. For most Moscow homes, what I typically end up recommending looks like this:

  • A properly sized softener for hardness, set to regenerate based on actual water use rather than a fixed timer. With the region's aquifers under long-term watch, it makes sense not to waste water or salt on unnecessary regenerations. More on that on the water softener page.
  • A reverse osmosis system at the kitchen sink for drinking and cooking water — it handles chlorine taste, the extra sodium from softening, and dissolved minerals in one place. Details are on the RO drinking water page.
  • Carbon filtration where it helps. If chlorine taste or smell bothers you at every faucet and shower — not just the kitchen — a whole-house carbon stage ahead of the softener is worth talking about. It isn't automatic; some households don't notice chlorine at all.

What I skip on city water: salt-free "conditioners," magnetic descalers, and oversized multi-tank packages built for problem wells. Never too much, never too little.

New builds, owner homes, and rentals near campus

New construction. If you're building or buying new in one of Moscow's newer subdivisions, the cheapest time to plan for treatment is before the drywall goes up. A softener loop with a nearby drain and outlet, and a line run for RO to the kitchen and refrigerator, cost very little during rough-in and save a lot of rework later. I design new-construction systems and can talk with your builder or plumber about where things should go.

Owner-occupied homes. Older Moscow houses sometimes have a softener that's been sitting in bypass for years, or a timer-based unit regenerating on a schedule that has nothing to do with the current household. Often the right move is to test, then decide whether to service, reprogram, or replace — not to assume you need all new equipment.

Rentals near the University of Idaho. Moscow has a lot of rental housing, and renters generally can't add plumbed-in equipment without the owner's approval. If you're a landlord, a softener protects water heaters, fixtures, and dishwashers that you're paying to replace, and it's a durable, serviceable upgrade between tenants. If you're renting, talk to your landlord before anything gets installed.

Viola and rural Moscow-area wells

Once you're off city water — Viola on US-95 north of town, or the private wells scattered around Moscow's edges — you're on your own well and your own chemistry. Nobody is chlorinating or removing iron for you. The same basalt aquifers that give the city iron and manganese to deal with can show up in a private well at levels that stain fixtures and defeat a plain softener, and bacteria becomes a question you have to answer with a test, not assume. For those homes I run a fuller test panel and design around it; the iron and manganese removal page covers that side in more detail.

How to check at home: Look at the aerators on your kitchen and bathroom faucets and the inside of your electric kettle. White, chalky crust is hardness. A brown or black film is more likely iron or manganese — on city water, that usually traces back to floc in the mains, especially right after the city's spring or fall flushing. If brown water shows up during flushing week, run your cold water for a few minutes before doing laundry. If it shows up at other times, that's worth mentioning when you call.
