Well water

Why do Palouse wells run high in iron and manganese, and what's the difference between them?

Most rural wells here draw from fractured basalt or the sediments between basalt flows. That groundwater is low in oxygen, so iron and manganese dissolve out of the rock and stay invisible until the water meets air. Iron shows up as orange-red staining and a metallic taste; it starts staining around 0.3 mg/L. Manganese leaves black or dark-brown slime and gray stains, starts staining as low as 0.05 mg/L, and at 0.3 mg/L is in EPA health-advisory territory. Manganese is also harder to remove, which is why many "iron filters" leave black staining behind. More on my iron and manganese removal page and in the Palouse well water guide.

My well tested positive for coliform. What now?

Any positive coliform result matters. It usually means surface water is finding a path into the well, and it's common on older wells, shallow casings, and after wet springs. Sometimes the fix is at the wellhead; sometimes continuous disinfection is warranted. Either way it changes the rest of the treatment design, so it needs to be accounted for from the start rather than bolted on later.

Why does my water smell like rotten eggs?

That smell is usually hydrogen sulfide. The first thing I check is whether it shows up on the hot side only or on both hot and cold — hot-only often points to the water heater, while both usually means it's coming from the well. Where it comes from changes the fix. See my sulfur smell page for more.

What are tannins, and why do they matter?

Tannins are organic material that can tint water a tea color that doesn't settle out. They can bind iron in ways that make standard iron filters underperform, and they're a frequent reason a previous system never really worked. Wells up the Highway 6 corridor tend to run heavier on tannins and manganese than wells closer to Moscow.

Will one filter fix everything?

Sometimes one piece of equipment really is the answer — a properly configured softener can often handle moderate dissolved iron, manganese, and hardness in one pass. But heavier iron, coliform, tannins, or pH outside roughly 6.8 to 8 can each call for another stage, in the right order. There's no single best filter; there's a best design for your well's numbers.

City water

Is Moscow city water hard?

Moscow's water comes from city wells drawing on two basalt aquifers, the Grande Ronde and the Wanapum, and it is treated with chlorine before distribution. USGS has described the area's groundwater as moderately hard. The City also notes its water carries iron and manganese that can react with chlorine and form reddish sediment, which is part of why it flushes its system. The City publishes an annual water quality report, and I test at your tap during the visit. More on my Moscow city water page.

Does a softener make sense on city water?

It can. It depends on the hardness at your tap and what you want to fix — scale on fixtures and water heaters, spotted dishes, dry skin and hair. I test first and tell you honestly whether a softener is worth it for your home. See my water softener page.

How do I get rid of the chlorine taste?

Activated carbon is the usual tool for chlorine taste and odor. It can be a whole-house stage or part of a drinking water system, depending on whether the taste only bothers you at the kitchen tap or everywhere.

Is reverse osmosis worth it?

For drinking and cooking water, an under-sink reverse osmosis system tailored to your water is one of the best upgrades you can make. It isn't a whole-house fix, and on hard or iron-heavy water the membrane needs the right pretreatment to last. See my reverse osmosis page.

Do I need a whole-house system or just drinking water?

It depends on your goals. Staining, scale, and odor throughout the house call for whole-house treatment; if the main concern is what you drink and cook with, a drinking water system may be enough. I'll walk you through practical options so you can choose what makes the most sense.

Salt-based softener or salt-free — and what about magnetic descalers?

A properly configured salt-based softener actually removes hardness, and on Palouse well water it can often take out dissolved iron and manganese too. Salt-free "conditioners" sold as softener alternatives don't deliver what the marketing implies on genuinely hard water with iron present. Magnetic and electronic descalers have no credible evidence behind them on water like ours, so I don't sell them.

Can I have a softener and reverse osmosis together?

Yes, and they work well together. The softener handles hardness for the whole house and protects the RO membrane from scale, and the RO system gives you drinking and cooking water at the kitchen sink. I size and place them as one design.
